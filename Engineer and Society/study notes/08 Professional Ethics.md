## 9. Professional Ethics

## Study Notes

### 1. 🧭 Understanding Professional Ethics: Defensible vs Correct Decisions

Professional ethics is about making decisions that are morally right and justifiable in a professional context. However, when faced with ethical dilemmas, it’s important to understand that there may not always be a **correct** decision. Instead, what we aim for is a **defensible decision**.

- **Ethical Dilemma:** A situation where two or more conflicting moral principles apply, and any choice has both positive and negative consequences.
- **Correct Decision:** A perfect choice that is entirely right without any downside. Often, such decisions don’t exist in complex ethical situations.
- **Defensible Decision:** A decision based on sound reasoning and ethical principles that can be justified and defended in any forum or discussion. It means you can explain why you made that choice, even if others might disagree.

The goal of ethical decision-making is to make decisions that are defensible, not necessarily perfect. This means carefully considering all aspects and being able to explain your reasoning clearly.


### 2. 🧪 Informal Ethical Tests: Quick Tools for Ethical Decision Making

Sometimes, we need to make quick ethical judgments without deep analysis. Informal tests are simple, intuitive checks that help us quickly assess whether an action might be ethical or not. These tests rely on our moral intuition and common sense.

- **Moral Intuition Test:** Your first impression or gut feeling about whether something feels right or wrong.
- **Shushers Test:** If you want to keep an action secret or hush it up, it’s likely unethical. For example, releasing buggy software but telling no one is suspicious.
- **Mom Test:** Would you be comfortable telling your mother about the action? If not, it might be unethical.
- **TV Test:** Would you be okay if your action was broadcast on TV? If you wouldn’t want it publicized, it might be unethical.
- **Market Test:** Consider your personal brand or reputation. Will this action enhance or damage how others see you?
- **Smell Test:** Does the situation “smell” bad or suspicious? If something feels off, it probably is.
- **Other Person’s Shoe Test:** Imagine yourself in the other person’s position. Would you find the action fair or just?

These informal tests are useful for quick checks but should be supplemented with more formal ethical analysis for important decisions.


### 3. ⚖️ Formal Ethical Theories: Frameworks for Deep Ethical Analysis

Formal ethical theories provide structured ways to analyze ethical dilemmas. They help us think systematically about what makes an action right or wrong.

#### Consequentialist (Teleological) Theories
- Focus on the **outcomes or consequences** of actions.
- The right action is the one that produces the best overall results.
- **Hedonism:** The pursuit of pleasure or happiness.
- **Egoism:** What is best for oneself.
- **Utilitarianism:** What produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number of people.
- Utilitarianism is widely used in ethical decision-making because it considers the overall good.

#### Deontological Theories
- Focus on **rights and duties** rather than consequences.
- Actions are right or wrong based on whether they respect the rights of stakeholders.
- Example: Human rights, animal rights, children’s rights.
- Every right has a corresponding duty (e.g., if someone has a right to safety, others have a duty to protect that safety).

#### Virtue Ethics
- Focus on the character and virtues of the person making the decision.
- Ask: *What would a virtuous person do?* (e.g., honesty, courage, fairness)
- Emphasizes moral character over rules or consequences.

#### Kant’s Categorical Imperative
Immanuel Kant proposed two key formulations:

1. **First Formulation:**  
   *“Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.”*  
   This means you should only act in ways that you would want everyone else to act. For example, you cannot make a promise you intend to break because if everyone did that, promises would become meaningless.

2. **Second Formulation:**  
   *“Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means.”*  
   This means treating people with respect and fairness, never using them just as tools to achieve your goals.

#### The Golden Rule
- Positive form: Treat others as you would like to be treated.
- Negative form (Silver Rule): Do not treat others in ways you would not want to be treated.

Good ethical theories help us build logical, persuasive arguments for our decisions.


### 4. 🛠️ Formal Guidelines: The Kallman and Grillo Four-Step Process for Ethical Decision Making

To make defensible ethical decisions, Kallman and Grillo propose a clear, step-by-step process:

#### Step 1: List and Understand the Facts
- Identify all relevant facts about the situation.
- Determine which facts raise ethical issues.
- Identify all stakeholders involved (people or groups affected).

#### Step 2: Isolate the Major Ethical Dilemma
- Clearly state the ethical dilemma as a question, usually framed as:  
  *“Should someone do or not do something?”*  
- Focus on the core ethical question without bias.

#### Step 3: Analyze the Ethicality of Both Alternatives
Use three ethical lenses to analyze the options:

- **Consequentialism:**  
  - Who will be harmed or benefited if the action is done or not done?  
  - Which option causes the least harm and the most benefit?

- **Rights and Duties:**  
  - What rights might be violated or respected?  
  - What duties are neglected or fulfilled?  
  - Identify stakeholders’ rights and corresponding duties.

- **Kant’s Categorical Imperative:**  
  - Who would be treated with disrespect or unfairly by each option?  
  - Would the action be acceptable if everyone did it?  
  - Which alternative treats people as ends, not just means?

#### Step 4: Make the Decision and Plan Implementation
- Choose the defensible ethical decision based on the analysis.
- Justify your choice by referencing the ethical principles that support it.
- Address any conflicting rights or duties and explain which take precedence.
- Plan specific steps to implement the decision.
- Consider how stakeholders will be affected.
- Think about long-term changes (legal, political, organizational) to prevent similar dilemmas.
- Reflect on what could have been done differently to avoid the dilemma.


### 5. 🌍 Additional Ethical Considerations: Santa Clara University’s Ethical Thinking Guide

This guide offers a practical approach to ethical thinking:

1. **Greatest Good:**  
   Does the behavior promote the greatest good or human welfare?

2. **Rights and Duties:**  
   Identify legitimate rights of individuals or groups and the duties of those involved.

3. **Fairness:**  
   Are all parties treated fairly? Equal treatment unless there is a justified reason for difference.

4. **Virtues:**  
   Is the behavior consistent with recognized virtues like honesty, courage, and respect?

5. **Common Good:**  
   Does the behavior serve the common good, benefiting society as a whole?


### Summary

Professional ethics is about making decisions that can be justified and defended, even when there is no perfect answer. Quick informal tests help us make snap judgments, but formal ethical theories and structured processes like the Kallman and Grillo four-step method provide a deeper, more reliable way to analyze dilemmas. By considering consequences, rights, duties, virtues, and universal principles, we can make ethical decisions that respect all stakeholders and promote fairness and the common good.
