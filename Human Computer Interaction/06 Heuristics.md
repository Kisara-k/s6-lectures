## Study Notes

### 1. 🧠 Introduction to Heuristics in Usability

When designing or evaluating user interfaces, one of the key approaches is to use **heuristics**. Heuristics are essentially rules of thumb, guidelines, or principles that help designers and evaluators judge how usable a system is. They are not strict rules but practical advice based on experience and research. Sometimes, this process is called **heuristic evaluation**.

There are many heuristics out there—some are grounded in theory, while others come from practical experience or vendor-specific guidelines. Because there are so many, this study focuses on two well-known and influential sets of heuristics: **Nielsen’s 10 heuristics (1994)** and **Shneiderman and Plaisant’s 8 golden rules (2010)**. Understanding these will give you a solid foundation for evaluating and improving user interfaces.


### 2. 📋 Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics (1994)

Jakob Nielsen, a pioneer in usability engineering, created a list of ten heuristics that are widely used to evaluate user interfaces. These heuristics are broad principles that help ensure a system is easy to use and understand. Here’s a detailed explanation of each:

1. **Visibility of system status**  
   The system should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time. For example, if a file is loading, the system should show a progress bar or spinner so users know it’s working.

2. **Match between system and the real world**  
   The system should speak the users’ language, using familiar words, phrases, and concepts rather than technical jargon. It should follow real-world conventions to make information appear in a natural and logical order.

3. **User control and freedom**  
   Users often make mistakes or want to backtrack. The system should provide clearly marked “emergency exits” so users can undo or redo actions easily without feeling trapped.

4. **Consistency and standards**  
   Users should not have to wonder whether different words, situations, or actions mean the same thing. The system should follow platform conventions and maintain consistency throughout.

5. **Error prevention**  
   Even better than good error messages is a careful design that prevents problems from occurring in the first place. For example, disabling irrelevant options or confirming destructive actions.

6. **Recognition rather than recall**  
   Minimize the user’s memory load by making objects, actions, and options visible. Users should not have to remember information from one part of the interface to another.

7. **Flexibility and efficiency of use**  
   The system should cater to both inexperienced and experienced users by providing shortcuts or accelerators for expert users, while still being easy for beginners.

8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design**  
   Interfaces should not contain irrelevant or rarely needed information. Every extra unit of information competes with the relevant units and diminishes their visibility.

9. **Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors**  
   Error messages should be expressed in plain language (no codes), precisely indicate the problem, and constructively suggest a solution.

10. **Help and documentation**  
    Even though it’s better if the system can be used without documentation, sometimes help is necessary. This help should be easy to search, focused on the user’s task, list concrete steps to be carried out, and not be too large.

You can find the full list and explanations on Nielsen’s website: [Nielsen’s Heuristics](http://www.useit.com/papers/heuristic/heuristic_list.html).


### 3. 🌟 Shneiderman and Plaisant’s 8 Golden Rules (2010)

Ben Shneiderman and Catherine Plaisant proposed another influential set of usability principles called the **8 Golden Rules**. These are practical guidelines aimed at creating user-friendly interfaces. Here’s a detailed look at each:

1. **Strive for consistency**  
   Similar actions and elements should behave the same way throughout the system. This helps users build a mental model and predict what will happen.

2. **Cater to universal usability**  
   Design for a wide range of users, including those with different abilities, ages, and experience levels. This means providing multiple ways to perform tasks and accommodating different preferences.

3. **Offer informative feedback**  
   Every user action should result in some feedback, so users know their input was received and what the system is doing.

4. **Design dialogues to yield closure**  
   Sequences of actions should have a clear beginning, middle, and end. For example, after completing a form, the system should confirm submission so users know the task is done.

5. **Prevent errors**  
   Like Nielsen’s heuristics, this rule emphasizes designing interfaces that reduce the chance of user errors.

6. **Permit easy reversal of actions**  
   Users should be able to undo or redo actions easily, which encourages exploration and reduces anxiety.

7. **Support internal locus of control**  
   Users want to feel in charge of the system and that the system responds to their actions, not the other way around.

8. **Reduce short-term memory load**  
   Interfaces should minimize the amount of information users need to remember by making options and instructions visible.

These rules are explained in detail in their book *Designing the User Interface* (pp. 88-89).


### 4. 🔍 Comparing Nielsen’s and Shneiderman & Plaisant’s Heuristics

Both sets of heuristics aim to improve usability but approach it slightly differently:

- **Nielsen’s heuristics** are more focused on specific usability issues like error prevention, recognition vs. recall, and minimalist design.
- **Shneiderman and Plaisant’s rules** emphasize broader design philosophies such as universal usability, closure in dialogues, and supporting user control.

Together, they provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating and designing user interfaces. You should be able to explain and apply both sets in practical usability evaluations.


### 5. 🖥️ Vendor-Specific Guidelines

Beyond these general heuristics, many companies provide their own **vendor-specific guidelines** tailored to their platforms. These are often more prescriptive and focused on the look and feel of their systems. Examples include:

- **Mac OS X Human Interface Guidelines**  
  Apple’s guidelines focus on creating consistent, intuitive experiences on Mac systems. They cover everything from layout to interaction patterns.  
  [Apple HIG](http://developer.apple.com/library/mac/#documentation/UserExperience/Conceptual/AppleHIGuidelines/Intro/Intro.html)

- **Microsoft Official User Interface Guidelines**  
  Microsoft provides extensive documentation for Windows applications, emphasizing consistency and usability within the Windows ecosystem.  
  [Microsoft UI Guidelines](http://msdn.microsoft.com/en-us/library/Aa511327)

These vendor guidelines are useful when designing for specific platforms but are more restrictive and less general than Nielsen’s or Shneiderman’s heuristics.


### 6. 🔎 Usability Inspection Methods

Usability inspection is a way to evaluate a user interface without involving actual users. Instead, **experts** analyze the interface based on their knowledge and experience. This method is often called **expert evaluation** and is considered a “cheap and cheerful” way to find usability problems early.

#### How usability inspection differs from user studies:

- **User studies** involve real users performing tasks to see where they struggle.
- **Usability inspection** involves experts reviewing the interface to identify potential issues.

Two main types of usability inspection are:

- **Heuristic evaluation** (covered here)
- **Cognitive walkthrough** (not covered in this module)


### 7. ✅ Heuristic Evaluation Explained

Heuristic evaluation was developed by Jakob Nielsen in the late 1980s as a quick and effective way to find usability problems.

- **Process:** Experts review the interface using a set of heuristics (like Nielsen’s 10 heuristics).
- **Scope:** It’s a holistic evaluation, meaning experts look at the entire system rather than focusing on specific tasks.
- **Effectiveness:** Research shows that a single expert may miss many issues, but when 3-5 experts evaluate the system independently, the combined findings cover most usability problems.

Key papers on heuristic evaluation include:

- Nielsen and Molich (1990): Introduced heuristic evaluation and showed its effectiveness.  
  [Link to paper](http://dx.doi.org/10.1145/97243.97281)

- Nielsen and Landauer (1993): Developed a mathematical model predicting how many evaluators are needed to find most usability problems. They found that about **four evaluators** provide the best cost-benefit balance.  
  [Link to paper](http://dx.doi.org/10.1145/169059.169166)

- Bevan et al. (2003): Questioned the “magic number 5” evaluators for some web testing scenarios, suggesting that more evaluators might be needed depending on the context.  
  [Link to paper](http://dx.doi.org/10.1145/765891.765936)


### Summary

Heuristics are essential tools in usability evaluation, providing practical guidelines to improve user interfaces. Nielsen’s 10 heuristics and Shneiderman and Plaisant’s 8 golden rules are foundational sets that cover a wide range of usability principles. Vendor-specific guidelines add more detailed, platform-focused advice. Usability inspection, especially heuristic evaluation, is a cost-effective expert method to identify usability problems early in the design process. Understanding and applying these heuristics will help you create more user-friendly and effective systems.
