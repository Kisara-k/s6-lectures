## 7. Design Rules

## Study Notes

### 1. 🎯 Introduction to Design Rules in Interaction Design

When designing interactive systems—like websites, apps, or software—the ultimate goal is to make them **highly usable**. Usability means that users can easily learn, efficiently use, and effectively achieve their goals with the system. To reach this goal, designers rely on **design rules**, which are structured guidelines and principles that help create user-friendly interfaces.

Design rules come in different forms and levels of detail, authority, and generality. Understanding these rules helps designers create systems that are intuitive, flexible, and robust, ensuring a positive user experience. This study note will explain the types of design rules, key usability principles, and how these rules guide the design process.


### 2. 📚 Types of Design Rules: Principles, Standards, and Guidelines

Design rules can be categorized into three main types, each with different characteristics:

- **Principles**: These are **abstract, broad rules** that apply generally across many design situations. They have **low authority** (meaning they are more like recommendations than strict rules) but **high generality** (they apply to many contexts). For example, a principle might be "make the system predictable."

- **Standards**: These are **specific, formal rules** often set by official organizations (like ISO). They have **high authority** (must be followed) but **limited application** (apply to specific technologies or industries). For example, hardware interface standards or ISO usability standards.

- **Guidelines**: These fall between principles and standards. They are **more specific than principles** but **less strict than standards**. They have **lower authority** but **broader application**. Guidelines often come from best practices or expert recommendations and can be adapted to different projects.

#### Visualizing Authority and Generality

- **Principles**: High generality, low authority  
- **Guidelines**: Medium generality, medium authority  
- **Standards**: Low generality, high authority  

This means principles guide broad thinking, standards enforce strict rules, and guidelines offer flexible advice.


### 3. 🧠 Principles to Support Usability

Usability is supported by three main principles: **Learnability**, **Flexibility**, and **Robustness**. Each principle focuses on different aspects of how users interact with a system.

#### Learnability

Learnability is about how easy it is for new users to start using the system effectively and reach their goals quickly. Key ideas include:

- **Predictability**: Users should be able to guess what will happen next based on their past actions. For example, if clicking a button usually opens a menu, users expect that behavior consistently.

- **Synthesizability**: Users need to understand the effects of their past actions. This means the system should provide clear feedback immediately or eventually, so users can assess what they did.

- **Familiarity**: The system should leverage users’ prior knowledge. For example, using common icons or layouts helps users guess how to use new features.

- **Generalizability**: Users should be able to apply what they learn in one part of the system to other parts or similar systems.

- **Consistency**: Similar tasks or situations should behave in similar ways, reducing confusion and learning time.

#### Flexibility

Flexibility refers to the system’s ability to support different ways of interaction and adapt to user needs:

- **Dialogue Initiative**: Users should have freedom in how they interact, rather than being forced into rigid system-driven dialogues.

- **Multithreading**: The system should allow users to work on multiple tasks at once, either concurrently or by switching between tasks smoothly.

- **Task Migratability**: The system and user can share responsibility for tasks. For example, the system might automate some steps but allow the user to intervene.

- **Substitutivity**: Users should be able to use different inputs or outputs interchangeably. For example, entering data via keyboard or voice.

- **Customizability**: Users or the system can modify the interface to better suit individual preferences or needs.

#### Robustness

Robustness is about supporting users in successfully completing tasks and recovering from errors:

- **Observability**: Users should be able to see the system’s internal state through clear, perceivable feedback. This includes easy navigation, visible defaults, and persistent information.

- **Recoverability**: Users must be able to correct mistakes easily, with options to undo or redo actions and minimal effort to recover.

- **Responsiveness**: The system should communicate quickly and smoothly, so users feel in control.

- **Task Conformance**: The system should fully support all the user’s tasks, ensuring completeness and adequacy.


### 4. 🛠 Using Design Rules in Practice: Standards and Guidelines

Design rules are not just theoretical—they guide real design work. Here’s how standards and guidelines function:

#### Standards

- Created by official bodies (e.g., ISO) to ensure consistency across many designers and products.
- Require solid theoretical backing and stable technology.
- More common in hardware but increasingly important in software.
- Example: **ISO 9241** defines usability as effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction.

#### Guidelines

- More flexible and suggestive than standards.
- Found in textbooks, reports, and style guides.
- Can be **abstract** (high-level principles used early in design) or **detailed** (specific style guides used later).
- Understanding why a guideline exists helps resolve conflicts when multiple guidelines apply.


### 5. ✅ Golden Rules and Heuristics: Quick Checks for Good Design

Golden rules and heuristics are broad, practical design rules that help designers quickly evaluate and improve usability. They are not strict laws but useful checklists.

#### Shneiderman’s 8 Golden Rules

1. **Strive for consistency**: Make similar operations behave similarly.
2. **Enable frequent users to use shortcuts**: Allow experienced users to speed up.
3. **Offer informative feedback**: Always tell users what’s happening.
4. **Design dialogs to yield closure**: Clearly indicate when tasks are complete.
5. **Offer error prevention and simple error handling**: Avoid errors and make recovery easy.
6. **Permit easy reversal of actions**: Allow undoing mistakes.
7. **Support internal locus of control**: Users should feel in charge, not the system.
8. **Reduce short-term memory load**: Don’t overload users with information to remember.

#### Norman’s 7 Principles

1. Use both **knowledge in the world** (external cues) and **knowledge in the head** (memory).
2. Simplify the structure of tasks to reduce complexity.
3. Make things visible to bridge the gap between what users want to do and what the system shows.
4. Get the mappings right—ensure controls correspond logically to their effects.
5. Exploit constraints to guide user actions naturally.
6. Design for error by anticipating and mitigating mistakes.
7. When all else fails, standardize to reduce confusion.


### 6. 🧩 HCI Design Patterns: Reusing Successful Solutions

Design patterns are reusable solutions to common design problems, originally from architecture but now widely used in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI).

- A **pattern** solves a recurring problem in a specific context.
- Patterns are **invariant**, meaning they work reliably across different situations.
- Examples:
  - Architecture: “Light on Two Sides of Every Room” ensures balanced lighting.
  - HCI: “Go back to a safe place” means providing a way to return to a known state in the interface.

#### Characteristics of Design Patterns

- Capture **practical design knowledge**, not just theory.
- Represent essential features of good design examples.
- Work at different levels: social, organizational, conceptual, and detailed.
- Reflect values like usability and humane design.
- Are intuitive and easy to communicate among designers, developers, and stakeholders.
- Form **pattern languages** that help create complete, coherent designs by linking patterns together.


### 7. 📝 Summary: The Role of Design Rules in Usability

Designing for usability is about creating systems that are easy to learn, flexible to use, and robust in supporting users. This requires:

- Abstract **principles** to guide thinking.
- Concrete **standards** to ensure compliance and consistency.
- Practical **guidelines** to inform detailed design decisions.
- Use of **golden rules and heuristics** as quick checks.
- Leveraging **design patterns** to reuse proven solutions.

Good design balances **creative insight** with **principled practice**, using these rules to build interfaces that truly serve users’ needs.
