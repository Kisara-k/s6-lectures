## 2. The Computer

## Study Notes

### 1. 💻 Understanding the Computer System: The Basics

To truly grasp how humans interact with computers, it’s essential to understand what a computer system is made of and how its components work together. A computer system consists of several key elements that influence how users input data, how the computer processes it, and how output is delivered back to the user.

#### Components of a Computer System

- **Input Devices:** These are tools that allow users to enter data into the computer. Examples include keyboards for text entry, mice or touchpads for pointing, scanners for digitizing paper documents, and sensors like microphones or bio-sensors.
  
- **Output Devices:** These display or present information from the computer to the user. Common output devices include screens (both small like smartphones and large like monitors), printers (digital paper), and special devices like virtual reality headsets.

- **Memory:** This refers to where data is stored temporarily or permanently. RAM (Random Access Memory) is fast but temporary, while permanent media (like hard drives or SSDs) store data long-term. Memory capacity and access speed are crucial for performance.

- **Processing:** The computer’s processor (CPU) executes instructions and processes data. Processing speed affects how quickly tasks are completed.

- **Networks:** Computers often connect to other devices and systems via networks, enabling resource sharing and communication.

#### Why This Matters for Interaction

Human-computer interaction depends on understanding what goes in (input), what the computer can do (processing and memory), and what comes out (output). This knowledge helps design better interfaces and interactions.


### 2. ⏳ From Batch Processing to Interactive Computing

Computing has evolved dramatically over time. Originally, computers operated in **batch processing** mode:

- Users prepared large stacks of punched cards or data files.
- They submitted these batches to the computer and waited a long time for results.
- Output was printed on line printers, and if errors occurred, the process had to be repeated.

Today, most computing is **interactive**:

- Users receive rapid feedback.
- They control the process in real-time.
- Interaction is dynamic and ongoing, allowing users to do tasks rather than just think about them.

#### Is Faster Always Better?

While speed is important, richer interaction often matters more. For example, having sensors everywhere and multiple input devices allows more natural and varied ways to interact with computers.


### 3. ⌨️ Text Entry Devices: Keyboards and Alternatives

#### Keyboards: The Most Common Text Input Device

- Keyboards allow fast text entry for experienced users.
- Each keypress sends a character code to the computer.
- Usually connected by cable or wirelessly.
- The **QWERTY** layout is standard but not optimal for typing speed; it was designed to prevent typewriter jams.
- Minor variations exist between UK and US keyboards, and different scripts require accented symbols.

#### Alternative Keyboard Layouts

- **Alphabetic Layout:** Keys arranged alphabetically; not faster for beginners or experts.
- **Dvorak Layout:** Designed to place common letters under the strongest fingers and alternate hands for common letter pairs, improving speed by 10-15% and reducing fatigue. However, social resistance keeps QWERTY dominant.

#### Special Keyboards

- Designed to reduce repetitive strain injury (RSI).
- One-handed keyboards like the Maltron for left-handed users.

#### Chord Keyboards

- Use combinations of a few keys to produce letters.
- Compact and portable.
- Require training but can be fast.
- Limited adoption due to social resistance and fatigue.

#### Phone Pads and T9 Entry

- Numeric keys represent multiple letters (e.g., 2 = a, b, c).
- Press keys multiple times to select letters.
- T9 predictive text guesses words from key sequences, speeding up typing.

#### Handwriting Recognition

- Uses a pen and digitizing tablet.
- Captures stroke path, pressure, and segments joined writing.
- Challenges include interpreting different handwriting styles.
- Common in PDAs and tablets.

#### Speech Recognition

- Rapidly improving.
- Works best with single users, initial training, and limited vocabularies.
- Challenges include background noise, pronunciation differences, and large vocabularies.


### 4. 🖱️ Pointing, Positioning, and Drawing Devices

#### The Mouse

- A handheld device that moves a cursor on the screen.
- Detects relative movement on a flat surface.
- Usually has 1-3 buttons for selection or drawing.
- Two main motion detection methods:
  - **Mechanical:** A ball rolls on the surface, turning potentiometers.
  - **Optical:** Uses LEDs and sensors to detect movement via reflected light.
- Advantages: Accurate, fast, and doesn’t obscure the screen.
- Disadvantages: Requires desk space and can cause hand-eye coordination issues for beginners.

#### Touchpads

- Small, touch-sensitive surfaces mainly on laptops.
- Users stroke to move the cursor.
- Acceleration settings help balance speed and precision.

#### Trackballs and Thumbwheels

- **Trackball:** A ball rotates inside a fixed housing; cursor moves accordingly.
- **Thumbwheels:** Small dials for precise cursor control or scrolling.

#### Joysticks and Keyboard Nipples

- Joysticks control cursor velocity via stick pressure; common in gaming and 3D navigation.
- Keyboard nipples are tiny joysticks embedded in keyboards for cursor control.

#### Touchscreens

- Detect finger or stylus contact.
- Advantages: Fast, direct, no extra device needed.
- Disadvantages: Finger marks, imprecise for small targets, arm fatigue.

#### Stylus and Light Pen

- Stylus: Pen-like device for direct screen drawing.
- Light Pen: Detects screen light to locate position (rarely used now).
- Both are direct but can obscure the screen.

#### Eyegaze Tracking

- Uses low-power lasers to track eye direction.
- Enables hands-free control.
- High accuracy requires headsets; cheaper versions exist.

#### Cursor Keys

- Four directional keys on keyboards.
- Cheap but slow for navigation.
- Common layout is inverted “T”.


### 5. 🖥️ Display Devices: Screens and Digital Paper

#### Bitmap Screens

- Made up of thousands or millions of tiny colored dots called pixels.
- **Resolution:** Number of pixels horizontally and vertically (e.g., 1024x768).
- **Pixel Density:** Pixels per inch (dpi), typically 72-96 dpi on screens.
- **Aspect Ratio:** Width to height ratio, commonly 4:3 or 16:9.
- **Color Depth:** Number of colors per pixel, from black/white to millions of colors (24-bit color).

#### Anti-aliasing

- Technique to smooth jagged edges on diagonal lines by blending colors.

#### Cathode Ray Tube (CRT)

- Uses electron beams to light phosphor dots on the screen.
- Bulky and heavy but was standard for many years.

#### Liquid Crystal Displays (LCD)

- Thin, light, and radiation-free.
- Used in PDAs, laptops, and increasingly desktop monitors and TVs.
- Work by controlling light polarization through crystals.
- Reflect ambient light, reducing eye strain.

#### Digital Paper

- Flexible, thin sheets that display images electronically.
- Retain the image without power.
- Use tiny spheres or liquid channels to change appearance.
- An emerging technology for portable reading and note-taking.


### 6. 🕶️ Virtual Reality and 3D Interaction

#### Positioning in 3D Space

- Devices like steering wheels, knobs, and 3D mice allow control in three dimensions.
- Data gloves use fiber optics to detect finger positions.
- VR helmets track head motion and sometimes eye gaze.
- Whole-body tracking uses accelerometers or reflective markers.

#### VR Headsets

- Provide separate screens for each eye with slightly different images to create 3D effects.
- Can cause motion sickness due to lag between head movement and display update, and conflicting depth cues.

#### Simulators and VR Caves

- Use projected scenes on walls, hydraulic motion platforms, and real controls.
- Provide immersive environments for training and entertainment.


### 7. 🔊 Physical Interaction: Sound, Touch, and Bio-sensing

#### Physical Controls and Dedicated Displays

- Include dials, gauges, lights, and small LCD or LED screens.
- Head-up displays in aircraft show critical information in the pilot’s line of sight.

#### Sounds

- Used for alerts, confirmations, and feedback.
- Examples: beeps, clicks, whistles.

#### Touch and Haptics

- Haptic devices provide force feedback and vibrations.
- Important in gaming and simulations (e.g., surgical instruments).
- BMW iDrive uses haptic feedback to help users select menu items by feel.

#### Environmental and Bio-sensing

- Sensors detect environmental conditions (light, temperature, weight).
- Bio-sensors monitor body data like heart rate, skin response, and iris patterns.
- Used for security, health monitoring, and adaptive interfaces.


### 8. 🖨️ Paper: Printing and Scanning

#### Printing Technology

- Prints images made of tiny dots.
- Key features:
  - **Resolution:** Dots per inch (dpi).
  - **Speed:** Pages per minute.
  - **Cost:** Important for choosing printer type.

#### Types of Printers

- **Dot-matrix:** Pins strike an inked ribbon; low resolution (~80-120 dpi).
- **Ink-jet:** Sprays tiny ink droplets; better resolution (~300 dpi).
- **Laser:** Uses electrostatic charge and toner; high resolution (600+ dpi).

#### Fonts

- Styles of text (e.g., Courier, Helvetica, Times Roman).
- Size measured in points (1 pt ≈ 1/72 inch).
- Fixed-pitch fonts have equal character widths; variable-pitch fonts vary widths.
- Serif fonts have decorative ends; sans-serif fonts have plain ends.

#### Page Description Languages

- Describe complex pages with fonts, images, and graphics.
- PostScript is a common language used to send instructions to printers.

#### Scanning and OCR

- Scanners convert paper into digital bitmaps.
- Flat-bed scanners digitize whole pages; hand-held scanners digitize strips.
- Optical Character Recognition (OCR) converts scanned images back into editable text.
- OCR faces challenges with different fonts and page layouts.


### 9. 💾 Storage, Processing, and Performance

#### Compression

- Reduces storage space.
- **Lossless:** Exact original data recovered (e.g., ZIP, GIF).
- **Lossy:** Approximate data recovered, exploiting human perception (e.g., JPEG, MP3).

#### Storage Formats

- **Text:** ASCII (7-bit), UTF-8 (8-bit), RTF (formatted text), SGML/XML (structured documents).
- **Media:** Various image (JPEG, TIFF), audio (WAV, MP3), and video (MPEG, QuickTime) formats.

#### Processing Speed and Limitations

- Faster processors allow more complex interfaces.
- Problems arise if processing is too slow (lag, unresponsive UI) or too fast (text scrolling too quickly).
- Moore’s Law predicts processor speed doubles roughly every 18 months, memory capacity every 12 months.

#### Bottlenecks

- **Computation bound:** Slow processing frustrates users.
- **Storage channel bound:** Slow data transfer between disk and memory.
- **Graphics bound:** Display updates require heavy processing.
- **Network capacity:** Slow networks reduce interactive performance.


### Summary

Understanding the computer system’s components—input devices, output devices, memory, processing, and networks—is fundamental to designing effective human-computer interactions. From traditional keyboards and mice to advanced VR and bio-sensing, each device offers unique ways to interact. Display technologies range from CRTs to digital paper, while printing and scanning bridge the digital and physical worlds. Performance depends on processing speed, storage, and network capacity, all evolving rapidly thanks to technological advances like Moore’s Law. This foundational knowledge helps us appreciate the complexity and possibilities of modern computing.
