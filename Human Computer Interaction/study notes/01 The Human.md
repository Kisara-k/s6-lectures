## 1. The Human

## Study Notes

### 1. 👤 Understanding The Human: An Overview

When we talk about "The Human" in the context of information processing and interaction, we are essentially looking at how humans receive, store, process, and apply information. This involves multiple senses and cognitive functions working together. Humans take in information through senses like vision, hearing, and touch, store this information in different types of memory, and then use reasoning and problem-solving skills to act on it. Emotions also play a crucial role by influencing how well we perform these tasks. Importantly, every person is unique, with differences in perception, memory, and emotional response.


### 2. 👁️ Vision: How We See and Interpret the World

Vision is one of the primary ways humans gather information. It involves two main stages:

#### Physical Reception of Visual Stimuli
The eye acts like a camera, receiving light reflected from objects around us. This light is focused upside-down on the retina at the back of the eye. The retina contains two types of cells:

- **Rods:** Sensitive to low light, helping us see in dim conditions.
- **Cones:** Responsible for color vision, sensitive to different wavelengths of light.

After light hits these cells, ganglion cells send electrical signals to the brain, which detects patterns and movement.

#### Processing and Interpretation of Visual Signals
Once the brain receives signals, it interprets them to understand size, depth, brightness, and color.

- **Size and Depth:** The visual angle tells us how much of our view an object takes up, which helps estimate its size and distance. Our brain uses cues like overlapping objects to judge depth. Even if an object’s size on our retina changes (like when it moves farther away), we still perceive it as the same size because of familiarity.
  
- **Brightness:** This is our subjective experience of light levels. It depends on the luminance (actual light emitted or reflected by an object). Our ability to see detail (visual acuity) improves with brightness, and we can detect flickering better in brighter conditions.

- **Color:** Color perception depends on hue (the type of color), intensity (brightness), and saturation (color purity). Cones in the retina are sensitive to different wavelengths corresponding to colors. Blue is the hardest color for humans to see clearly, and some people (mostly males) are color blind.

#### Compensation and Illusions
Our visual system adjusts for movement and changes in light to maintain a stable view. Sometimes, this compensation leads to optical illusions, where what we see doesn’t match reality. Examples include:

- **Ponzo Illusion:** Lines appear different lengths due to background cues.
- **Müller-Lyer Illusion:** Lines with arrowheads appear longer or shorter.

#### Reading and Vision
Reading is a complex visual process involving:

- Recognizing visual patterns (letters and words).
- Decoding these patterns using our internal knowledge of language.
- Interpreting meaning based on syntax (grammar), semantics (meaning), and pragmatics (context).

Our eyes move in quick jumps called saccades, pausing briefly (fixations) to process information. The shape of words and contrast (like negative contrast on screens) affect how easily we read.


### 3. 👂 Hearing: Understanding Sound and Its Role

Hearing helps us understand our environment by providing information about distance, direction, and the nature of sounds.

#### The Ear’s Structure and Function
- **Outer Ear:** Protects the inner ear and amplifies sound.
- **Middle Ear:** Converts sound waves into vibrations.
- **Inner Ear:** Translates vibrations into chemical signals that trigger impulses in the auditory nerve.

#### Characteristics of Sound
- **Pitch:** Determined by frequency (how fast sound waves vibrate).
- **Loudness:** Determined by amplitude (wave height).
- **Timbre:** The quality or type of sound (e.g., a violin vs. a piano).

Humans hear frequencies roughly between 20 Hz and 15,000 Hz, with better sensitivity to lower frequencies. Our auditory system can filter sounds, allowing us to focus on specific sounds even in noisy environments, a phenomenon known as the "cocktail party effect."


### 4. ✋ Touch and Movement: Feeling and Acting in the World

#### Touch
Touch provides critical feedback about our surroundings and is especially important for people with visual impairments. The skin contains different receptors:

- **Thermoreceptors:** Detect heat and cold.
- **Nociceptors:** Detect pain.
- **Mechanoreceptors:** Detect pressure, either instant or continuous.

Some body parts, like fingertips, are more sensitive than others. 

#### Kinesthesia
This is our awareness of body position and movement, which affects how comfortable and effective we are in performing tasks.

#### Movement and Reaction Time
Movement involves two components:

- **Reaction Time:** The delay between stimulus and start of movement.
- **Movement Time:** The time taken to complete the movement.

Reaction time varies by stimulus type:

- Visual: ~200 milliseconds
- Auditory: ~150 milliseconds
- Pain: ~700 milliseconds

Interestingly, skilled people can maintain accuracy even with slower reaction times, while unskilled people’s accuracy decreases.

#### Fitts’ Law
This law predicts how long it takes to move to a target, such as clicking a button on a screen. The formula is:


$$
Mt = a + b \log_2 \left(\frac{D}{S} + 1\right)
$$


Where:
- $Mt$ = movement time
- $D$ = distance to target
- $S$ = size of target
- $a$ and $b$ are constants based on empirical data

The takeaway: targets should be as large as possible and as close as possible to reduce movement time.


### 5. 🧠 Memory: How We Store and Recall Information

Memory is essential for learning and functioning. It comes in three main types:

#### Sensory Memory
This is a very brief buffer for sensory input:

- **Iconic Memory:** Visual information.
- **Echoic Memory:** Auditory information.
- **Haptic Memory:** Touch information.

Sensory memory holds information just long enough for us to decide if it’s important, then it’s overwritten quickly.

#### Short-Term Memory (STM) or Working Memory
STM acts like a scratch-pad for information we need immediately. It has:

- Very fast access (~70 milliseconds).
- Very rapid decay (~200 milliseconds).
- Limited capacity, typically 7 ± 2 chunks of information (like digits or words).

For example, remembering a phone number briefly before dialing.

#### Long-Term Memory (LTM)
LTM stores all our knowledge and experiences:

- Access is slower (~1/10 second).
- Decay is very slow or negligible.
- Capacity is huge or unlimited.

LTM has two main types:

- **Episodic Memory:** Memory of events and experiences.
- **Semantic Memory:** Memory of facts, concepts, and skills.

Semantic memory is structured and supports inference, meaning we can connect pieces of information logically.

#### Models of Long-Term Memory

- **Semantic Networks:** Information is stored as nodes connected by relationships. Child nodes inherit properties from parent nodes, allowing us to make inferences.
  
- **Frames:** Data structures with slots filled by specific values. For example, a "dog" frame might have fixed properties like "4 legs" and variable properties like "color."

- **Scripts:** Represent typical sequences of events in a situation, like visiting a vet. Scripts include roles, props, scenes, and tracks to help us understand and predict what happens.

- **Production Rules:** Condition-action pairs used to represent procedural knowledge. For example, "If dog is wagging tail, then pat dog."

#### Memory Processes
- **Rehearsal:** Repeating information to move it from STM to LTM.
- **Forgetting:** Happens due to decay (slow loss over time) or interference (new info replacing old or vice versa).
- **Retrieval:** Can be recall (actively reproducing info) or recognition (identifying info as familiar).


### 6. 🤔 Thinking: Reasoning and Problem Solving

Thinking involves reasoning and solving problems using knowledge.

#### Types of Reasoning

- **Deductive Reasoning:** Drawing logically necessary conclusions from premises. For example, "If it is Friday, she will go to work. It is Friday, so she will go to work." However, logical validity doesn’t guarantee truth if premises are false.

- **Inductive Reasoning:** Generalizing from specific cases to broader rules. For example, "All elephants I’ve seen have trunks, so all elephants have trunks." This is useful but not foolproof.

- **Abductive Reasoning:** Inferring the most likely cause from an observed effect. For example, "Sam drives fast when drunk. I see Sam driving fast, so I assume he is drunk." This can lead to false conclusions.

#### Problem Solving

Problem solving is finding solutions to new or unfamiliar tasks. There are several theories:

- **Gestalt Theory:** Emphasizes insight and restructuring the problem to find solutions.
- **Problem Space Theory:** Views problem solving as moving through a space of possible states using operators (actions). Heuristics like means-ends analysis help select the best moves.
- **Analogy:** Using knowledge from similar problems to solve new ones, though this is harder when domains differ.
- **Skill Acquisition:** Skilled problem solvers chunk information to optimize working memory and group problems conceptually.


### 7. ⚠️ Errors and Mental Models

Humans make errors, which can be categorized as:

- **Slips:** The right intention but wrong execution, often due to inattention or poor skill.
- **Mistakes:** Wrong intention due to incorrect understanding.

People create mental models to explain how things work. If these models are incorrect, errors occur.


### 8. ❤️ Emotion: How Feelings Affect Human Performance

Emotion is a complex mix of cognitive and physical responses to stimuli. There are several theories:

- **James-Lange:** Emotion is our interpretation of physiological responses.
- **Cannon:** Emotion is a psychological response.
- **Schacter-Singer:** Emotion results from evaluating physiological responses in context.

Emotion influences how we think and act:

- Positive emotions promote creative problem solving.
- Negative emotions narrow thinking and can make even simple tasks harder.

For example, stress increases difficulty in problem solving, while relaxed users are more forgiving of design flaws.


### 9. 👥 Individual Differences and Design Implications

People differ in many ways that affect how they interact with systems:

- **Long-term differences:** Sex, physical and intellectual abilities.
- **Short-term differences:** Stress, fatigue.
- **Changing factors:** Age.

Designers must consider these differences to avoid excluding parts of the user population. For example, since blue acuity is poor, blue should not be used for critical details in interfaces.


### 10. 🖥️ Psychology and Interactive System Design

Understanding human perception, memory, reasoning, and emotion is essential for designing effective interactive systems. While some guidelines exist (like avoiding blue for important details), good design requires understanding the psychological context and experimental evidence.

Key tools include:

- **Guidelines:** Practical rules distilled from research.
- **Cognitive Models:** Theoretical frameworks explaining human information processing.
- **Evaluation Techniques:** Experimental and analytic methods to test designs.


### Summary

This lecture covers the fundamental ways humans perceive, remember, think, and feel, and how these processes affect interaction with systems. Vision, hearing, touch, memory, reasoning, problem solving, errors, emotion, and individual differences all play vital roles. Understanding these helps create better, more inclusive designs that work well for diverse users.
