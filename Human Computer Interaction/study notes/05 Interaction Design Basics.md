## 5. Interaction Design Basics

## Study Notes

### 1. 🎨 What is Design? Understanding the Foundations

Design is about **achieving goals within constraints**. It’s not just about making something look good but about solving problems and creating useful, usable, and enjoyable experiences for users.

- **Goals**: These are the purposes behind the design. Who is the design for? What do they want to achieve? For example, a warehouse manager might want a system that helps her track inventory quickly and easily.
- **Constraints**: These are the limits within which the design must work. Constraints can be physical (materials), technical (platforms, software), or social (user skills, cultural expectations).
- **Trade-offs**: Because of constraints, designers often have to balance competing needs. For example, making a system very powerful might make it harder to use.

#### The Golden Rule of Design

To design well, you must **understand your materials**. In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), this means:

- Understanding **computers**: their capabilities and limitations.
- Understanding **people**: their psychology, social context, and how they make errors.
- Understanding the **interaction** between people and computers.

#### Human Error is Normal

People make mistakes — it’s part of being human. Instead of blaming users for errors, good design anticipates these errors and builds systems that help prevent or recover from them. Just like engineers design buildings to withstand stress, interaction designers must design systems that accommodate human behavior under stress.


### 2. 🔄 The Design Process: From Idea to Reality

Design is a **process**, not a one-time event. It involves several stages:

- **Requirements**: Understanding what currently exists and what is needed.
- **Analysis**: Organizing and making sense of the requirements.
- **Design**: Deciding what to do and how to do it.
- **Iteration and Prototyping**: Creating early versions, testing, and improving repeatedly.
- **Implementation and Deployment**: Building the final product and releasing it to users.

#### Why Iteration Matters

You almost never get the design right the first time. Prototyping and testing help uncover what users really need and what works best. This cycle of **prototype → evaluate → redesign** is essential.

#### Managing Trade-offs

Designers often face limited time and resources, so they must prioritize what to focus on. Usability testing helps find problems, but deciding which problems to fix requires judgment.


### 3. 👥 Knowing Your Users: The Heart of Interaction Design

Designing without understanding users is like shooting in the dark. You need to **know who your users are**, what they want, and how they behave.

#### Personas: Imaginary Users with Real Details

A **persona** is a detailed description of a typical user. It’s not a real person but a realistic character that helps designers keep the user in mind.

**Example Persona: Betty**

- 37 years old, Warehouse Manager for 5 years.
- Has some computer experience but limited formal education.
- Has a physical limitation (restricted right-hand movement).
- Enthusiastic but wary of new computer systems.

Using personas helps designers ask, “What would Betty think or do here?” This keeps design decisions user-focused.

#### Cultural Probes: Learning from Real Life

Sometimes direct observation is hard, so designers use **cultural probes** — small kits given to users to record their experiences in their own environment. For example, a camera or a postcard might prompt users to share what matters to them. This helps designers understand users’ real contexts and feelings.


### 4. 📖 Scenarios: Stories that Bring Design to Life

Scenarios are **rich, detailed stories** about how users might interact with a system. They help designers and stakeholders understand the user’s goals, actions, and thoughts step-by-step.

#### Why Use Scenarios?

- They communicate ideas clearly to others.
- They validate other models and assumptions.
- They help explore the dynamics of interaction over time.

#### Example Scenario: Movie Player

Brian wants to watch a new film and invite his friend Alison, who dislikes “arty” films. He downloads the movie on his work computer, then transfers it to his personal movie player using Bluetooth. The scenario describes each step Brian takes, what he sees on the screen, and what he thinks.

#### Acting Out Scenarios

Designers can **role-play** scenarios to explore how users might interact with a device or system. This helps uncover hidden problems and understand user cognition.

#### Limitations of Scenarios

Scenarios usually show one linear path through a system, which is easy to understand but doesn’t capture all possible choices or errors. Using multiple scenarios helps cover different possibilities.


### 5. 🧭 Navigation Design: Helping Users Find Their Way

Navigation is about how users **move through a system** — whether it’s a website, an app, or a physical device.

#### Levels of Navigation

- **Local navigation**: What can you do on this screen? What options are available here?
- **Global navigation**: How do you move between different parts of the system or site?
- **Wider navigation**: How does this system relate to other applications or the web?

#### Four Golden Rules of Navigation

1. **Know where you are**: Users should always understand their current location.
2. **Know what you can do**: Clear options and actions should be visible.
3. **Know where you are going**: Users should understand what will happen next.
4. **Know where you’ve been**: Users should be able to see their path or history.

#### Tools for Navigation

- **Breadcrumbs**: Show the path through a website hierarchy.
- **Menus and buttons**: Should be clear and consistent.
- **Hierarchical diagrams**: Show the structure of the system.
- **Network diagrams**: Show possible paths and branches.

#### Avoiding Navigation Pitfalls

- Deep hierarchies are hard to navigate.
- Too many options on one screen can overwhelm users.
- Consistency and clarity are key.


### 6. 🖥️ Screen and Interface Design: Making Information Clear and Usable

Good screen design helps users understand and interact with information easily.

#### Basic Principles

- **Grouping**: Related items should be physically close.
- **Structure and order**: Items should follow a natural, logical order.
- **Alignment**: Text and controls should be aligned for easy scanning.
- **White space**: Use gaps to separate and highlight groups.

#### Examples

- Group billing details together, separate from delivery details.
- Align text left for easy reading (in English).
- Align numbers by decimal points or right-align for easy comparison.
- Use boxes and fonts to emphasize headings but avoid clutter.

#### Decoration and Aesthetics

- Use fonts, colors, and boxes to guide attention.
- Avoid overusing colors or 3D effects, which can confuse or tire users.
- Consider color blindness and cultural differences in color meanings.


### 7. 🎛️ Physical Controls and User Actions: Designing for Interaction

Physical controls (buttons, knobs, dials) must be designed with the same care as screens.

#### Key Points

- Group related controls together (e.g., defrost settings, cooking time).
- Order controls logically (e.g., type of heating → temperature → time → start).
- Use color and lines to differentiate functions.
- Align text on buttons for easy scanning.
- Leave enough white space to avoid accidental presses.

#### Affordances: What Controls Suggest

Affordances are cues that tell users how to interact with something:

- A button looks “clickable.”
- A knob suggests twisting.
- A handle suggests grasping.

On screens, icons and buttons should visually suggest their function, but remember that digital affordances differ from physical ones.


### 8. 📝 Entering Information and User Control

When users input data, the interface must guide them clearly.

#### Forms and Dialog Boxes

- Use logical layout based on task analysis.
- Group related fields.
- Follow natural reading order (top to bottom, left to right).
- Set tab order for keyboard users.

#### Knowing What to Do

- Make active areas clear (where to click or type).
- Use consistent styles (e.g., underlined links).
- Use clear labels and icons.


### 9. 🌈 Presenting Information: Balancing Aesthetics and Utility

Information presentation must be both **useful and pleasing**.

#### Purpose Matters

- Choose the right format (text, graphs, tables) based on what users need.
- Allow interactivity (e.g., sorting columns).

#### Aesthetics and Utility

- Attractive designs improve satisfaction and productivity.
- Avoid mixing too many visual styles.
- Ensure readability (avoid backgrounds behind text).
- Use color and 3D effects sparingly and meaningfully.

#### Localization and Internationalization

- Design for different languages and cultures.
- Use symbols that work globally.
- Be aware of cultural differences in color and symbols (e.g., ticks and crosses).


### 10. 🔄 Prototyping and Iteration: Improving Through Feedback

Prototyping is about creating early versions of a design to test ideas and find problems.

#### Why Prototype?

- You rarely get it right the first time.
- Prototypes help you understand what works and what doesn’t.
- Iteration means **prototype → evaluate → redesign** repeatedly.

#### Pitfalls

- Moving slowly without a clear goal can waste time.
- You need a good starting point and a clear understanding of what to improve.


### Summary

Interaction design is a complex but rewarding process focused on creating systems that work well for people. It requires understanding users deeply, designing thoughtfully, testing often, and balancing many factors like usability, aesthetics, and technical constraints. By following structured processes, using tools like personas and scenarios, and iterating through prototypes, designers can create effective and enjoyable interactive experiences.
