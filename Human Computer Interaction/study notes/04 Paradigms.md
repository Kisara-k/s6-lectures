## 4. Paradigms

## Study Notes

### 1. 🔍 Introduction to Paradigms in Interactive System Design

When studying interactive systems—like the software and devices we use daily—it's important to understand **paradigms**. Paradigms are the big-picture frameworks or worldviews that shape how we think about and design these systems. They guide what kinds of systems we build, how we expect users to interact with them, and how we measure their success, especially in terms of **usability**.

Two key concerns in interactive system design are:

- **How can we develop interactive systems that are easy and effective for people to use?**
- **How can we demonstrate or measure that a system is indeed usable?**

The history of interactive system design is essentially a story of **paradigm shifts**—major changes in how we think about and build these systems. By studying these shifts, we gain insight into why systems look and behave the way they do today, and how future systems might evolve.


### 2. 🧠 What Are Paradigms?

A **paradigm** is a dominant theoretical framework or worldview that shapes scientific thinking and practice. For example, in physics, we have had paradigms like Aristotelian physics, Newtonian mechanics, and Einstein’s relativity. Each paradigm changes how scientists understand the world.

In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), paradigms represent the evolving ways we understand the relationship between humans and computers. As new technologies emerge, they bring new paradigms that redefine this relationship.

Understanding HCI history means understanding these paradigm shifts—some of which are well-established, others still debated. These shifts reflect changes in technology, user needs, and design philosophy.


### 3. 🖥️ Paradigms of Interaction: A Historical Overview

The history of interactive computing can be traced through a series of paradigm shifts, each marked by new technologies and new ways of thinking about human-computer interaction.

#### The Initial Paradigm: Batch Processing

- Early computers operated in **batch processing** mode.
- Users submitted jobs (programs) and waited for results without interaction during processing.
- This was an **impersonal** form of computing, with no direct user interaction during execution.

#### Time-Sharing and Interactive Computing

- The **time-sharing** paradigm emerged in the 1960s.
- It allowed multiple users to interact with a single computer simultaneously.
- This shift made computing more **interactive**, enabling users to give commands and receive immediate feedback.

#### Networking and Community Computing

- The rise of **networking** connected multiple computers, enabling communication and resource sharing.
- This led to **community computing**, where groups of users could collaborate and share information electronically.

#### Graphical Displays and Direct Manipulation

- The introduction of **graphical displays** transformed interaction from text-based commands to visual interfaces.
- **Direct manipulation** paradigms allowed users to interact with objects on the screen directly (e.g., dragging files).
- This made computing more intuitive and accessible.

#### Microprocessors and Personal Computing

- The development of the **microprocessor** enabled powerful, affordable personal computers.
- This shifted computing from large shared systems to individual ownership and use.

#### The World Wide Web and Global Information

- The **WWW** paradigm connected computers worldwide, creating a global information space.
- It transformed how people access, share, and create information.

#### Ubiquitous Computing: The Future Vision

- The latest paradigm is **ubiquitous computing**, where computing is embedded seamlessly into everyday objects and environments.
- The goal is for technology to **disappear** into the background, supporting daily activities without demanding constant attention.


### 4. ⏳ Time-Sharing and Early Interactive Systems

The 1940s and 1950s saw rapid technological growth, but computers were still large and expensive. By the 1960s, there was a need to make computing more accessible.

- **J.C.R. Licklider** at ARPA was a key figure advocating for interactive computing.
- Time-sharing allowed multiple users to share a single computer’s resources, making computing more efficient and interactive.
- This was a foundational shift from batch processing to interactive use.


### 5. 🖼️ Video Display Units and Visual Interaction

Paper was the dominant medium for information, but computers began to use **video display units (VDUs)** to show data visually.

- In 1962, **Sutherland’s Sketchpad** demonstrated how computers could be used for visualizing and manipulating data.
- This was a major step toward graphical user interfaces, allowing users to interact with visual objects rather than just text.


### 6. 🛠️ Programming Toolkits and Augmenting Human Intellect

Douglas Engelbart at the Stanford Research Institute pioneered tools to **augment human intellect**.

- In 1963, Engelbart began developing systems to help humans work better with computers.
- The 1968 NLS/Augment system demonstrated how programming toolkits could provide building blocks for complex interactive systems.
- These toolkits made it easier to create sophisticated user interfaces and interactive applications.


### 7. 🧒 Personal Computing and the Dynabook Vision

The 1970s brought the idea that computers should be **personal and easy to use**.

- Seymour Papert developed the **LOGO language**, enabling children to program simple graphics.
- Alan Kay at Xerox PARC envisioned the **Dynabook**, a small, powerful personal computer designed for individual use.
- The focus shifted to making systems more powerful by making them easier and more intuitive for users.


### 8. 🪟 Window Systems and the WIMP Interface

Humans often multitask, so interfaces needed to support multiple simultaneous activities.

- **Window systems** allowed users to partition their screen into multiple areas, each representing a different task or application.
- The **WIMP** interface (Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers) became a standard interaction style.
- The Xerox Star (1981) was the first commercial system to use windows extensively.
- These elements are now familiar parts of modern graphical user interfaces.


### 9. 🪞 Metaphors in Interaction Design

Using metaphors helps users understand new technology by relating it to familiar real-world activities.

- Examples include the **desktop metaphor** (files and folders like paper on a desk), **word processing as typing**, and **spreadsheets as financial ledgers**.
- Metaphors make learning easier but can also limit understanding if the metaphor doesn’t fit all tasks or carries cultural biases.
- Virtual reality is an example where the user is placed **inside** the metaphor.


### 10. 🖱️ Direct Manipulation: Action Over Language

In 1982, Ben Shneiderman described the appeal of **direct manipulation** interfaces:

- Objects on the screen are visible and can be acted upon directly.
- Actions provide immediate feedback, encouraging exploration.
- Users can undo actions, reducing fear of mistakes.
- This approach replaces complex command languages with intuitive actions.
- The 1984 Apple Macintosh popularized this model, emphasizing **What You See Is What You Get (WYSIWYG)**.


### 11. 🗣️ Language vs. Action in Interfaces

While direct manipulation emphasizes action, language still plays a role:

- Some interfaces act as **mediators**, translating user commands into system actions.
- Programming by example combines both action and language, allowing users to demonstrate tasks rather than write code.
- Intelligent agents and natural language processing bring language back into interfaces, making them more proactive and conversational.


### 12. 🔗 Hypertext and Non-Linear Information

Hypertext allows users to navigate information non-linearly, following links rather than reading sequentially.

- Vannevar Bush’s 1945 **memex** concept envisioned managing information explosion.
- Ted Nelson coined the term **hypertext** in the 1960s, describing linked, non-linear browsing.
- Hypermedia extends this to include multimedia (images, video, sound).
- Nelson’s Xanadu project aimed to create a universal hypertext system but remains unrealized.


### 13. 🎤 Multimodality: Multiple Communication Channels

Humans communicate through many channels—speech, gesture, touch, vision.

- **Multimodality** in computing means using multiple input and output modes simultaneously.
- This can make interaction more natural and flexible, for example, combining voice commands with touch input.


### 14. 🤝 Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)

CSCW focuses on systems that support groups rather than individuals.

- It recognizes the **social aspects** of computing.
- Electronic mail is a prime example of CSCW success, enabling asynchronous communication.
- CSCW systems support collaboration, coordination, and communication among users.


### 15. 🌐 The World Wide Web: A Global Information Paradigm

The Web transformed hypertext from a closed system into a global, open platform.

- Simple protocols like **HTTP** and markup languages like **HTML** made publishing and accessing information easy.
- The Web’s rapid growth created a **critical mass** of users, transforming the information economy.
- It enabled new forms of communication, commerce, and knowledge sharing.


### 16. 🤖 Agent-Based Interfaces: Intelligent Interaction

Agent-based interfaces bring intelligence and proactivity to user interaction.

- Early interfaces were command-based, requiring users to remember and type commands.
- Direct manipulation replaced commands with actions on visible objects.
- Agents combine the two by understanding natural language and anticipating user needs.
- Examples include avatars and virtual assistants that act on behalf of users.


### 17. 🌍 Ubiquitous Computing: Technology That Disappears

Mark Weiser famously said, “The most profound technologies are those that disappear.”

- In the late 1980s, computers were very visible and demanding of attention.
- Ubiquitous computing aims to **embed computing into everyday objects and environments** so seamlessly that it fades into the background.
- The goal is to design interactions that don’t require constant user focus.


### 18. 📡 Sensor-Based and Context-Aware Interaction

Humans are excellent at understanding context—knowing where they are, what’s happening, and how to respond.

- Advances in sensors allow computers to detect physical phenomena like light, temperature, location, and identity.
- The challenge is to translate these raw sensor inputs into meaningful, context-aware interactions.
- This enables systems that adapt automatically to their environment and user needs, making interaction more natural and efficient.


### Summary

The study of paradigms in interactive system design reveals how our relationship with computers has evolved—from batch processing to ubiquitous computing. Each paradigm shift reflects new technologies and new ways of thinking about usability and interaction. Understanding these shifts helps us design better systems that are more intuitive, effective, and integrated into our lives.
