## 12. Ubiquitous Computing and AR

## Key Points

#### 1. 💻 Ubiquitous Computing  
- Ubiquitous computing fills the real world with computers, allowing human interaction away from a single workstation.  
- Mark Weiser stated, “The most profound technologies are those that disappear.”  
- Ubiquitous computing changes technology, applications, and interaction theories.

#### 2. 📏 Device Scales in Ubiquitous Computing  
- Inch-scale devices include PDAs, smartphones, and voice recorders, typically owned personally and always carried.  
- Foot-scale devices include notebooks, tablets, and digital paper, owned personally but not always carried.  
- Yard-scale devices include electronic whiteboards and plasma displays, owned by institutions and shared by many.

#### 3. 🖥️ Interaction Experience in Ubiquitous Computing  
- Implicit input uses sensor-based data to extend explicit input like keyboard and mouse.  
- Recognition technologies introduce ambiguity because they are not perfect.  
- Output can be ambient (environmental cues) or peripheral (background displays), e.g., The Dangling String and The Water Lamp.

#### 4. 🌐 Merging Physical and Digital Worlds  
- Physical actions can have electronic meaning, and digital output can be superimposed on the physical world.  
- Examples include digital desks and augmented calendars.

#### 5. 📱 Application Themes  
- Context-aware computing uses sensed phenomena to facilitate interaction.  
- Automated capture stores live experiences for future access.  
- Continuous interaction means everyday activities have no clear start or end.

#### 6. 🔍 Theoretical Frameworks for Ubiquitous Computing  
- Knowledge in the world emphasizes physical surroundings as information sources.  
- Activity theory focuses on fluid adjustment of goals and actions to the physical world.  
- Situated action and distributed cognition highlight improvisation over planned actions.  
- Ethnography provides deep contextual understanding of activities.

#### 7. 🕶️ Virtual Reality (VR) Technology  
- VR uses headsets with small screens for each eye to create a 3D immersive effect.  
- Gesture recognition is achieved with devices like DataGlove.  
- VR includes visual, sound, haptic, and gesture inputs for life-like experiences.  
- Desktop VR uses ordinary monitors and PCs, is cheaper but less immersive.  
- VRML (Virtual Reality Markup Language) enables VR scenes on the web.

#### 8. 🕶️ Augmented Reality (AR)  
- AR overlays virtual images onto the real world via head-up displays, semi-transparent goggles, or desktop projections.  
- AR information can be unrelated (e.g., wearable email) or related (virtual objects interacting with real ones).  
- Registration (alignment of virtual and real) is critical for AR effectiveness.  
- AR applications include maintenance (overlaying instructions) and aircraft wiring schematics.

#### 9. 📊 Data Visualization in VR and AR  
- VR can visualize invisible scientific fields, e.g., virtual wind tunnels showing airflow.  
- Hierarchies can be visualized in 3D using cone trees to manage wide organizational charts.  
- Networks are represented as nodes and links; planar graphs can be drawn without crossing links, non-planar cannot.  
- Time-based data visualization includes timelines, Gantt charts, and interactive replay.

#### 10. 🌍 Real-World Examples  
- Ambient Wood uses real wood with sensors measuring light and moisture, data sent via GPRS and visualized on maps.  
- Shared virtual experiences allow people in different locations to interact via microphones and VR.

#### 11. ⚙️ Differences Between VR, AR, and Ubiquitous Computing  
- Ubiquitous computing fills the real world with invisible computers.  
- Virtual reality simulates the real world inside a computer for immersive experiences.  
- Augmented reality mixes virtual and real worlds by overlaying digital information on physical environments.
