## 5. Interaction Design Basics

## Key Points

#### 1. 🎨 Design Fundamentals  
- Design is about achieving goals within constraints.  
- Goals define the purpose and target users; constraints include materials and platforms.  
- Trade-offs are necessary due to constraints.  
- Understanding materials means knowing computers (limitations, tools) and people (psychological, social aspects, human error).  
- Human error is normal and should be designed for, not blamed.

#### 2. 🔄 Design Process  
- The design process includes requirements, analysis, design, iteration/prototyping, implementation, and deployment.  
- Iteration and prototyping are essential because the first design is rarely correct.  
- Usability testing helps find problems and decide what to fix.  
- Limited time forces design trade-offs.

#### 3. 👥 User Focus  
- Knowing your user is critical; users are often different from the designer.  
- Personas are detailed, fictional user descriptions used as surrogate users in design.  
- Cultural probes are tools given to users to record meaningful experiences in their environment.  
- Direct observation can be difficult but valuable.

#### 4. 📖 Scenarios  
- Scenarios are detailed stories describing user interactions step-by-step.  
- They help communicate, validate models, and understand interaction dynamics.  
- Scenarios typically show one linear path and should be used in multiples to cover alternatives.  
- Role-playing scenarios helps explore interaction, cognition, and system architecture.

#### 5. 🧭 Navigation Design  
- Navigation involves local (single screen), global (whole system), and wider (between applications) structures.  
- Four golden rules of navigation: know where you are, what you can do, where you are going, and where you’ve been.  
- Breadcrumbs show the path through a website hierarchy.  
- Deep hierarchies are difficult to navigate; menu size should consider Miller’s 7 ± 2 rule (short-term memory limits).  
- Consistency and clarity in navigation are essential.

#### 6. 🖥️ Screen and Interface Design  
- Group related items physically together; maintain logical order matching user tasks.  
- Align text left for readability (in English); align numbers by decimal or right-align integers.  
- Use white space to separate and highlight groups.  
- Use fonts and boxes for emphasis but avoid excessive decoration.  
- Overuse of color and 3D effects can reduce usability; consider color blindness and cultural differences.

#### 7. 🎛️ Physical Controls  
- Group related controls and order them logically (e.g., heating type, temperature, time, start).  
- Use color and lines to differentiate functions.  
- Align text on buttons for easy scanning; use white space to avoid accidental presses.  
- Affordances are visual or physical cues that suggest how to interact with controls.

#### 8. 📝 User Input and Control  
- Forms and dialog boxes should have logical layout based on task analysis.  
- Group related fields and follow natural reading order (top-bottom, left-right).  
- Set tab order for keyboard navigation.  
- Active areas must be clearly indicated; consistent styles help users know where to click or type.

#### 9. 🌈 Presenting Information  
- Presentation format depends on user needs (text, graphs, tables).  
- Aesthetics improve satisfaction but must not compromise readability or utility.  
- Color should be used sparingly and meaningfully; beware of color blindness.  
- Localization requires adapting interfaces for language, culture, and symbols.  
- Symbols like ticks and crosses may have different meanings across cultures.

#### 10. 🔄 Prototyping and Iteration  
- Prototyping is necessary because initial designs are rarely perfect.  
- Iteration involves prototyping, evaluating, and redesigning repeatedly.  
- A good starting point and understanding of what is wrong are essential to avoid aimless iteration.
