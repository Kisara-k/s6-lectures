## Key Points

#### 1. 👁️ Vision  
- The retina contains rods for low light vision and cones for color vision.  
- Images are focused upside-down on the retina.  
- Visual angle indicates how much of the view an object occupies, relating to size and distance.  
- Visual acuity is the ability to perceive detail and improves with luminance.  
- Familiar objects are perceived as constant size despite changes in visual angle.  
- Blue color acuity is the lowest among colors.  
- About 8% of males and 1% of females are color blind.  
- The visual system compensates for movement and changes in luminance.  
- Optical illusions like the Ponzo and Müller-Lyer illusions occur due to overcompensation by the visual system.

#### 2. 📖 Reading  
- Reading involves saccades (quick eye movements) and fixations (pauses).  
- Perception occurs during fixations.  
- Word shape is important for word recognition.  
- Negative contrast (light text on dark background) improves reading on computer screens.

#### 3. 👂 Hearing  
- Humans hear frequencies from 20 Hz to 15 kHz.  
- Auditory system filters sounds to focus on important sounds over background noise (cocktail party phenomenon).  
- Outer ear protects and amplifies sound; middle ear transmits vibrations; inner ear converts vibrations to nerve impulses.  
- Pitch relates to sound frequency; loudness relates to amplitude; timbre is the quality of sound.

#### 4. ✋ Touch  
- Touch receptors include thermoreceptors (heat/cold), nociceptors (pain), and mechanoreceptors (pressure).  
- Some body areas, like fingers, are more sensitive to touch than others.  
- Kinesthesia is the awareness of body position and affects comfort and performance.

#### 5. 🏃 Movement  
- Reaction times: visual ~200 ms, auditory ~150 ms, pain ~700 ms.  
- Movement time depends on age and fitness.  
- Increasing reaction time decreases accuracy in unskilled operators but not in skilled operators.  
- Fitts' Law: $M_t = a + b \log_2(D/S + 1)$, where $M_t$ is movement time, $D$ is distance, and $S$ is target size.

#### 6. 🧠 Memory  
- Three types of memory: sensory memory, short-term (working) memory, and long-term memory.  
- Selection of stimuli for further processing depends on the level of arousal.  
- Rehearsal is required to maintain information in short-term memory.

## Study Notes

### 1. 👤 Understanding The Human as an Information Processor

Humans constantly receive, process, store, and apply information from their environment. This process involves multiple sensory inputs, memory systems, and cognitive functions. Understanding how humans interact with information helps us design better tools, interfaces, and environments that fit human capabilities and limitations.

Humans receive information through senses such as vision, hearing, and touch. This sensory information is first received and then stored in different types of memory: sensory memory, short-term memory, and long-term memory. After storage, the brain processes this information through reasoning, problem-solving, and skill application. Emotions also play a significant role by influencing how well we perform these cognitive tasks. Importantly, every individual is unique, so human capabilities vary widely.


### 2. 👁️ Vision: How We See and Interpret the World

Vision is one of the most complex and important senses. It involves two main stages:

- **Physical reception of the stimulus**: This is how the eye captures light and converts it into signals.
- **Processing and interpretation of the stimulus**: This is how the brain makes sense of the signals to understand what we see.

#### The Eye: Physical Reception

The eye acts like a camera, receiving light reflected from objects around us. This light is focused onto the retina at the back of the eye, but interestingly, the image formed on the retina is upside-down. The retina contains two types of photoreceptor cells:

- **Rods**: These are sensitive to low light levels and help us see in dim conditions.
- **Cones**: These detect color and work best in bright light.

Additionally, **ganglion cells** in the retina help detect patterns and movement, sending this information to the brain for further processing.

#### Interpreting Visual Signals

Once the eye captures the image, the brain interprets it using several cues:

- **Size and Depth**: The visual angle tells us how much of our field of view an object occupies. This helps us judge the size and distance of objects. For example, a familiar object looks the same size even if it appears smaller when far away. Overlapping objects also help us perceive depth.
  
- **Brightness**: This is our subjective experience of how light or dark something appears. It depends on the luminance (amount of light reflected) of the object. Our ability to see fine details (visual acuity) improves with brightness, and we can detect flickering better in brighter conditions.

- **Color**: Color perception depends on three properties:
  - **Hue**: The type of color (red, blue, green, etc.)
  - **Intensity**: How bright the color is
  - **Saturation**: How pure or vivid the color is

Cones in the retina are sensitive to different wavelengths corresponding to colors. Blue light is the hardest to see clearly, and some people (about 8% of males and 1% of females) are color blind, meaning they cannot distinguish certain colors.

#### Visual System Compensation and Illusions

Our visual system is smart and compensates for:

- Movement of objects or ourselves
- Changes in lighting conditions

However, sometimes this compensation leads to **optical illusions**, where what we see does not match reality. Examples include:

- **Ponzo illusion**: Lines appear different lengths due to background cues.
- **Müller-Lyer illusion**: Lines with arrowheads appear longer or shorter depending on the direction of the arrows.


### 3. 📖 Reading: From Seeing Words to Understanding Meaning

Reading is a complex process that involves several stages:

1. **Visual pattern perception**: Recognizing the shapes of letters and words.
2. **Decoding**: Using our internal knowledge of language to interpret these patterns.
3. **Interpretation**: Applying knowledge of syntax (sentence structure), semantics (meaning), and pragmatics (context) to understand the text.

When we read, our eyes do not move smoothly but make quick jumps called **saccades**, followed by brief pauses called **fixations**. We perceive words mainly during fixations. The overall shape of words helps us recognize them quickly, and interestingly, using negative contrast (light text on a dark background) can improve reading on computer screens.


### 4. 👂 Hearing: How We Detect and Understand Sounds

Hearing provides crucial information about our environment, such as the location and distance of sounds, and helps us identify objects and events.

#### The Ear: Physical Apparatus

The ear has three main parts:

- **Outer ear**: Protects the inner parts and helps amplify sound.
- **Middle ear**: Transmits sound waves as vibrations to the inner ear.
- **Inner ear**: Converts vibrations into chemical signals that trigger impulses in the auditory nerve, sending sound information to the brain.

#### Sound Characteristics

Sound has several important properties:

- **Pitch**: Related to the frequency of sound waves; higher frequency means higher pitch.
- **Loudness**: Related to the amplitude or strength of the sound waves.
- **Timbre**: The quality or type of sound that makes it unique (e.g., the difference between a piano and a violin playing the same note).

Humans can hear frequencies roughly between 20 Hz and 15,000 Hz, but we are better at distinguishing lower frequencies than very high ones.

#### Auditory Filtering

Our auditory system can filter sounds, allowing us to focus on important sounds even in noisy environments. This is known as the **cocktail party phenomenon**, where you can focus on one conversation despite background noise.


### 5. ✋ Touch: Feeling the World Around Us

Touch is a vital sense that provides feedback about our environment and is especially important for people with visual impairments.

#### Types of Touch Receptors

The skin contains different receptors that detect various stimuli:

- **Thermoreceptors**: Sense heat and cold.
- **Nociceptors**: Detect pain.
- **Mechanoreceptors**: Sense pressure, which can be instant or continuous.

Some parts of the body, like the fingertips, are more sensitive to touch than others.

#### Kinesthesia: Awareness of Body Position

Kinesthesia is the sense of knowing where our body parts are without looking. This awareness affects how comfortable we feel and how well we perform physical tasks.


### 6. 🏃 Movement: Responding to Stimuli

Movement involves two key time components:

- **Reaction time**: The time it takes to start responding after a stimulus.
- **Movement time**: The time it takes to complete the movement.

Reaction time varies depending on the type of stimulus:

- Visual stimuli: about 200 milliseconds
- Auditory stimuli: about 150 milliseconds (faster than visual)
- Pain stimuli: about 700 milliseconds (slowest)

Movement time depends on factors like age and fitness.

Interestingly, increasing reaction time tends to reduce accuracy for unskilled people but does not affect skilled operators as much.

#### Fitts' Law: Predicting Movement Time

Fitts' Law is a formula that predicts how long it takes to move to a target on a screen or in the real world:


$$
M_t = a + b \log_2 \left(\frac{D}{S} + 1\right)
$$


Where:

- $M_t$ = movement time
- $D$ = distance to the target
- $S$ = size of the target
- $a$ and $b$ = constants determined by experiments

This law tells us that to reduce movement time, targets should be as large as possible and as close as possible.


### 7. 🧠 Memory: How We Store and Use Information

Memory is essential for learning and applying knowledge. There are three main types of memory:

- **Sensory memory**: Holds sensory information for a very short time (milliseconds to seconds). It acts as a buffer for incoming sensory data.
- **Short-term memory (or working memory)**: Holds information temporarily for processing and manipulation. It requires rehearsal (repeating information) to keep it active.
- **Long-term memory**: Stores information for long periods, potentially a lifetime.

The selection of what information moves from sensory memory to short-term memory depends on the level of **arousal**—how alert or attentive a person is.


### Summary

This lecture covered how humans receive, process, and apply information through senses like vision, hearing, and touch, how movement and reaction times affect performance, and how memory systems store and manage information. Understanding these processes helps us appreciate human strengths and limitations, which is crucial for designing effective human-computer interactions, workplaces, and learning environments.
