## 6. The Interaction

## Key Points

#### 1. 🤝 Interaction Models  
- Interaction involves communication between user and system, including language and action.  
- Key terms: domain (area of work), goal (desired outcome), task (actions to achieve goal).  
- Donald Norman’s model has seven stages: goal establishment, intention formulation, action specification, action execution, system state perception, system state interpretation, and evaluation against goal.  
- The execution/evaluation loop repeats these stages continuously.  
- Gulf of Execution: gap between user’s intended actions and system’s allowed actions.  
- Gulf of Evaluation: gap between system’s output and user’s ability to interpret it.  
- Human errors: slips (correct goal, wrong action) and mistakes (wrong goal or misunderstanding).  

#### 2. 🔄 Abowd & Beale Interaction Framework  
- Interaction consists of four components: user, input, system, output.  
- Interaction is a translation between the languages of user and system.  
- Problems in interaction arise from translation issues between these components.  

#### 3. 🖐️ Ergonomics  
- Ergonomics studies physical characteristics of interaction, also called human factors.  
- Ergonomics defines standards for control arrangement, environment, health, and color use.  
- Industrial interfaces differ from office interfaces in data type, rate of change, and environment.  
- Glass interfaces replace traditional dials with screens and keypads, offering flexibility but losing physical context.  

#### 4. 🎮 Interaction Styles  
- Common styles: command line, menus, natural language, question/answer, form-fills, spreadsheets, WIMP, point and click, 3D interfaces.  
- Command line interfaces are efficient for experts but hard for novices.  
- Menus rely on recognition, often hierarchical, selectable by keyboard or mouse.  
- Natural language is intuitive but ambiguous and difficult to implement well.  
- Form-fills and spreadsheets are structured data entry methods; spreadsheets maintain data consistency via formulas.  
- WIMP stands for Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers, the default style for most desktop systems.  

#### 5. 🪟 WIMP Interface Elements  
- Windows: movable, resizable screen areas with scrollbars and title bars.  
- Icons: small images representing objects or actions, can be stylized or realistic.  
- Pointers: controlled by mouse or keyboard, used to select interface elements.  
- Menus: pull-down, pop-up, cascading, and contextual types; keyboard accelerators provide shortcuts.  
- Buttons: clickable regions invoking actions; special types include radio buttons and checkboxes.  
- Toolbars: lines of icons for quick access, often customizable.  
- Palettes: floating windows with tools, can be tear-off or pinned.  
- Dialogue boxes: pop-ups requesting information or showing messages.  

#### 6. 🗣️ Speech-Driven Interfaces and Look & Feel  
- Speech interfaces are improving but still face accuracy and dialogue management challenges.  
- Look and feel refers to the appearance and behavior of interface elements, varying between systems (e.g., MacOS vs Windows).  

#### 7. ⚠️ Error and Repair  
- Errors are inevitable; systems should help users detect and repair errors easily.  
- Example: booking systems confirm details step-by-step to avoid misunderstandings.  

#### 8. 🌍 Context in Interaction  
- Interaction is influenced by social and organizational context, including motivation, competition, and fear of failure.  
- Poor system design can cause frustration and reduce motivation.  

#### 9. 🎉 Experience and Engagement  
- Users want enjoyable experiences, not just functional systems.  
- Concepts like flow (balance between challenge and skill) and physical engagement are important.  
- Value perception is critical for user adoption.  

#### 10. ⚙️ Physical Design Constraints and Trade-offs  
- Physical design must consider ergonomic, physical, legal, safety, environmental, aesthetic, and economic constraints.  
- Trade-offs are often necessary, e.g., safety vs. ease of use.  

#### 11. 🔄 Fluidity and Affordance  
- Good physical controls reflect logical system states (affordance).  
- Controls should make system state visible and support natural inverse actions (e.g., twist controls).  

#### 12. 💡 Managing Value  
- Users adopt systems only if perceived value exceeds cost (time, money, effort).  
- Value includes work efficiency, fun, and benefits to others; cost includes learning effort and money.  
- People heavily discount future value and cost, preferring immediate benefits.  
- Organizational factors affecting value: coercion, enculturation, and emergence.

## Study Notes

### 1. 🤝 What is Interaction?

Interaction is the fundamental process where a user communicates with a system. At its simplest, it’s about exchanging information between a human and a computer or device. But interaction is more than just communication — it involves understanding *how* this communication happens, the physical and social context, and the styles or methods through which users and systems engage.

#### Key Ideas:
- **Communication between user and system:** This is the core of interaction, but it’s not just sending and receiving messages.
- **Language and action:** Interaction involves not only language (commands, inputs) but also actions and responses, which are explored in more depth in related topics.
- **Models of interaction:** These help us understand and design how users and systems work together.


### 2. 🧩 Models and Terms of Interaction

To study interaction, we use models that break down the process into understandable parts. These models help designers create better systems by clarifying what happens during interaction.

#### Important Terms:
- **Domain:** The area or field where the interaction takes place (e.g., graphic design).
- **Goal:** What the user wants to achieve (e.g., create a red triangle).
- **Task:** The steps or actions the user takes to reach the goal (e.g., select fill tool, click on triangle).

#### Donald Norman’s Model of Interaction:
Norman’s model focuses on the user’s perspective and breaks interaction into seven stages:

1. **Establish the goal:** The user decides what they want to do.
2. **Formulate intention:** The user plans how to achieve the goal.
3. **Specify actions:** The user decides what actions to perform on the interface.
4. **Execute action:** The user carries out the actions.
5. **Perceive system state:** The user observes how the system responds.
6. **Interpret system state:** The user makes sense of the system’s feedback.
7. **Evaluate system state:** The user compares the system’s response to their goal.

This cycle is called the **execution/evaluation loop** and repeats as the user interacts.

#### The Gulf of Execution and Gulf of Evaluation:
- **Gulf of Execution:** The gap between what the user wants to do and what the system allows them to do.
- **Gulf of Evaluation:** The gap between the system’s output and the user’s ability to understand it.

#### Human Errors:
- **Slips:** When the user knows the goal and understands the system but makes a wrong action by accident.
- **Mistakes:** When the user has the wrong goal or misunderstands the system.

Fixing slips usually means improving the interface design, while fixing mistakes requires better user understanding or training.


### 3. 🔄 Abowd and Beale’s Interaction Framework

This framework extends Norman’s model by focusing on the translation between four components:

- **User:** Has intentions and inputs.
- **Input:** The actions the user performs.
- **System:** Processes inputs and changes state.
- **Output:** The system’s response, which the user perceives.

Interaction is seen as a continuous translation between the languages of the user and the system. Problems in interaction often arise from difficulties in these translations.


### 4. 🖐️ Ergonomics: The Physical Side of Interaction

Ergonomics, also called human factors, studies the physical aspects of how people interact with systems. It’s about designing interfaces that fit human physical capabilities and limitations.

#### Examples of Ergonomics in Design:
- **Control arrangement:** Grouping controls by function or frequency of use.
- **Environment:** Designing seating or workspace to fit different body sizes.
- **Health considerations:** Positioning controls to avoid strain, managing lighting and noise.
- **Use of color:** Using colors like red for warnings, green for safe, while considering color blindness.

#### Industrial vs. Office Interfaces:
- **Office interfaces:** Usually textual, slow-changing data, clean environments.
- **Industrial interfaces:** Often numeric, fast-changing data, harsh or dirty environments (e.g., oil-soaked mouse).

#### Glass Interfaces:
Modern industrial interfaces use screens and keypads (glass interfaces) instead of traditional dials and knobs. They offer flexibility but can lose physical context and be more complex.


### 5. 🎮 Interaction Styles: How Users and Systems Communicate

Interaction style refers to the nature of the dialogue between user and system — how commands and responses are exchanged.

#### Common Interaction Styles:

- **Command Line Interface (CLI):** Users type commands directly. Efficient for experts but hard for novices.
- **Menus:** Lists of options visible on screen, easy to use because users recognize options rather than recall them.
- **Natural Language:** Using everyday language (spoken or typed). Intuitive but ambiguous and difficult to implement well.
- **Question/Answer Interfaces:** System guides users through questions, good for novices but limited in flexibility.
- **Form-Fills and Spreadsheets:** Structured data entry, with spreadsheets adding formulas and automatic updates.
- **WIMP (Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers):** The most common style, especially on desktops.
- **Point and Click:** Clicking icons or links, common in multimedia and web browsing.
- **Three-Dimensional Interfaces:** Virtual reality or 3D workspaces, adding depth and spatial interaction.


### 6. 🪟 Elements of WIMP Interfaces

WIMP interfaces are made up of several key components that users interact with:

- **Windows:** Independent areas on the screen that can be moved, resized, and overlapped.
- **Icons:** Small images representing objects or actions.
- **Pointers:** The cursor controlled by mouse or other devices to select items.
- **Menus:** Lists of options, which can be pull-down, pop-up, or hierarchical.
- **Buttons:** Clickable areas that perform actions.
- **Toolbars:** Rows of icons for quick access to common functions.
- **Palettes:** Small floating windows with tools or options.
- **Dialogue Boxes:** Pop-up windows that request information or provide messages.

#### Menu Types:
- **Pull-down:** From a menu bar.
- **Pop-up:** Contextual menus appearing near the pointer.
- **Cascading:** Hierarchical menus opening submenus.
- **Keyboard accelerators:** Shortcuts to menu items.


### 7. 🗣️ Speech-Driven Interfaces and Look & Feel

Speech interfaces are improving but still face challenges with accuracy and robust dialogue management. Good systems confirm understanding to avoid errors.

#### Look and Feel:
- Refers to the appearance and behavior of interface elements.
- Different systems (e.g., MacOS vs Windows) have distinct look and feel.
- Consistency in look and feel helps users learn and predict system behavior.


### 8. ⚠️ Error and Repair in Interaction

Errors are inevitable in interaction. Good systems help users detect and fix errors easily.

- **Error detection:** Clear feedback when something goes wrong.
- **Error repair:** Allow users to correct mistakes without frustration.
- Example: A booking system confirming details step-by-step to avoid misunderstandings.


### 9. 🌍 Context: Social, Organizational, and Motivational Factors

Interaction doesn’t happen in isolation. The social and organizational context affects how users behave and feel.

- **Social factors:** Desire to impress, competition, fear of failure.
- **Motivation:** Fear, ambition, self-satisfaction.
- **System inadequacies:** Can cause frustration and reduce motivation.


### 10. 🎉 Experience, Engagement, and Fun

Users don’t just want to *use* systems; they want to *enjoy* using them. Designing for experience means considering:

- **Flow:** A balance between challenge and skill that keeps users engaged.
- **Physical engagement:** How the system feels to use.
- **Value:** Users must perceive the system as valuable to invest effort.


### 11. ⚙️ Physical Design and Trade-offs

Physical design involves many constraints:

- **Ergonomic:** Controls must be easy to use.
- **Physical:** Size and placement of buttons.
- **Legal and safety:** Controls must prevent accidents.
- **Context:** Environment affects design (e.g., easy to clean surfaces).
- **Aesthetic:** The system should look good.
- **Economic:** Cost considerations.

Design often requires trade-offs, balancing conflicting needs (e.g., safety vs. ease of use).


### 12. 🔄 Fluidity and Affordance in Physical Controls

Good physical design reflects the logical state of the system:

- **Affordance:** Controls should suggest their function (e.g., a button looks pressable).
- **State visibility:** The control’s physical state should show the system’s state (e.g., on/off).
- **Inverse actions:** Controls that naturally reverse actions (e.g., twist left to decrease volume).

Examples include spring-back controls and rotary knobs that reveal internal state.


### 13. 💡 Managing Value in Interaction

People use systems only if the perceived value outweighs the cost (time, money, effort).

- **Value:** Helps get work done, is fun, or benefits others.
- **Cost:** Includes learning effort, money, time.
- **Discounting future value:** People prefer immediate benefits and may resist learning new systems.

#### Organizational Context:
- **Coercion:** People use systems because they must (e.g., job requirements).
- **Enculturation:** People adopt values through culture and support.
- **Emergence:** Systems designed so individual values align with organizational goals.


### 14. 📝 Summary: Designing for Interaction

If you want someone to use a system or do something:

- Make it easy for them.
- Understand their values and motivations.
- Design interfaces that fit physical, cognitive, and social needs.
- Provide clear feedback and error recovery.
- Consider the context and experience, not just functionality.
