## 6. Perception

## Study Notes

### 1. 👁️ Introduction to Perception and Visual Thinking

Perception is the process by which our brain interprets and makes sense of the information received from our senses, especially vision. It’s not just about passively seeing the world but actively constructing an understanding based on what our brain filters and focuses on. Visual thinking involves how we perceive (see), pay attention to, and cognitively process visual stimuli. This process is complex and involves different mental systems working together to help us navigate and understand our environment.

Dr. Sandareka Wickramanayake’s lecture introduces us to the fundamental concepts of perception, cognition, and attention, highlighting how our brain processes visual information through two main thinking systems described by Daniel Kahneman: System 1 and System 2.


### 2. ⚡ Fast and Slow Thinking: System 1 and System 2

Our brain processes information using two distinct systems:

- **System 1 (Fast Thinking):**  
  This system operates automatically and unconsciously. It’s fast, effortless, and intuitive, handling about 98% of our thinking. It works without self-awareness or deliberate control. For example, recognizing a face or reacting quickly to a situation happens through System 1. It’s like the brain’s autopilot, making quick judgments based on what it immediately sees or senses. Kahneman calls this “What you see is all there is,” meaning System 1 relies on the information currently available without questioning or seeking more.

- **System 2 (Slow Thinking):**  
  This system is slow, conscious, and deliberate. It requires effort and self-awareness, engaging in logical and skeptical thinking. System 2 is responsible for tasks that need careful analysis, like solving math problems or making complex decisions. It only accounts for about 2% of our thinking but is crucial for reasoning and controlling impulses. It actively seeks new or missing information to make informed decisions.

Together, these systems allow us to quickly react to our environment while also having the capacity for deep, rational thought when needed.


### 3. 👀 How Human Vision Works

Our eyes don’t capture a continuous, perfect image like a camera. Instead, they sample the environment about 3-4 times per second. The brain then builds a mental picture from these snapshots. This means:

- **We see with our mind, not just our eyes.**  
  What we perceive depends on what is actually there, but also on what we expect to see and where our attention is focused. For example, if you expect to see a friend in a crowd, your brain is more likely to notice them.

- **“What you see is what you need.”**  
  Our perception is goal-oriented; we tend to notice what is relevant or important to us at the moment.

- **Limited Visual Working Memory:**  
  Our brain can only hold about 3-4 simple shapes or objects in visual working memory at once. This limitation means we can’t process every detail in a scene simultaneously.


### 4. 🎯 Selective Attention: Focusing on What Matters

Selective attention is the brain’s ability to focus on certain parts of the visual environment while ignoring others. This is why we don’t perceive everything around us all the time.

- **Selective Attention Tests:**  
  Famous experiments like the **Basketball Pass Test** by Dan Simons and Chris Chabris show how people can miss obvious things (like a person in a gorilla suit walking through) when they are focused on counting basketball passes. This demonstrates how attention filters what we perceive.

- **The Door Study:**  
  Another experiment where people fail to notice when the person they are talking to is replaced by someone else during a brief interruption, showing how attention limits perception.

- **Multi-Colour Card Trick:**  
  This trick by Richard Wiseman highlights how our attention can be misdirected, causing us to miss changes in color or objects.

These examples emphasize that **we only perceive what our mind attends to**, and much of what happens outside our focus goes unnoticed.


### 5. 🌈 Color and Depth Perception

Our perception of color and depth is not fixed; it can be influenced by various factors:

- **Color Perception:**  
  The way we see colors can change depending on shadows and the color of the light source illuminating an object. For example, an object might look different under sunlight compared to artificial light because our brain adjusts color perception based on context.

- **Depth Perception:**  
  Depth perception allows us to judge distances and see the world in three dimensions. It relies on cues like the relative size of objects, shadows, and motion. Videos and demonstrations show how our brain interprets these cues to create a sense of depth.


### 6. 🧠 How We Process Visual Information: Insights from Stephen Few

Stephen Few (2009) explains that visual perception is:

- **Selective:**  
  We don’t pay attention to everything we see. Our brain is especially sensitive to contrast and changes in the visual field, which helps us notice important details.

- **Influenced by Knowledge:**  
  We tend to see what we know or expect. Familiar objects or patterns attract our attention more than unfamiliar ones.

- **Limited by Memory:**  
  Our short-term visual memory has a small capacity, limiting how much information we can process from an image at once.


### 7. ⚡ Pre-attentive vs. Post-attentive Perception

Perception happens in two stages:

- **Pre-attentive Perception:**  
  This is the immediate, automatic reaction to visual stimuli before conscious attention is involved. It happens very quickly and helps us detect basic visual features like color, shape, or movement without effort.

- **Post-attentive Perception:**  
  This is slower and requires conscious effort. It involves searching for patterns, interpreting complex information, or performing mental calculations to understand what we see.

Understanding these stages helps in designing visuals that are easy to interpret quickly.


### 8. 🧩 Gestalt Principles: How We Group Visual Elements

Gestalt principles explain how our brain organizes visual elements into groups or patterns. These principles are crucial in data visualization and design because they help viewers quickly understand complex information.

Key Gestalt principles include:

- **Similarity:**  
  Elements that share visual properties (like color or shape) are perceived as part of the same group.

- **Proximity:**  
  Elements that are close to each other are seen as related or grouped together.

- **Enclosure:**  
  Surrounding elements with a boundary or shape visually groups them.

- **Common Fate:**  
  Objects moving in the same direction and speed are perceived as a group.

- **Parallelism:**  
  Lines with similar slopes are visually associated.

- **Connectedness:**  
  Elements connected by lines or shapes are seen as related.

These principles help our brain quickly make sense of visual data by grouping related items.


### 9. 🎨 Pre-attentive Visual Attributes

Certain visual features are processed pre-attentively, meaning we notice them instantly without conscious effort. These attributes include:

- **Color Intensity and Hue:**  
  Brightness and color differences stand out immediately.

- **Line Width and Length:**  
  Variations in thickness or length are quickly noticed.

- **Spatial Position and Orientation:**  
  Where an object is located and its angle can draw attention.

- **Enclosure and Size:**  
  Objects enclosed by shapes or differing in size are easily distinguished.

- **Shape and Curvature:**  
  Different shapes or curves attract immediate attention.

- **3D Depth and Focus/Blur:**  
  Depth cues and focus effects help highlight important elements.

Using these attributes effectively can make visual information easier to understand at a glance.


### Summary

Perception is an active, selective process where our brain filters and interprets visual information using both fast, automatic thinking (System 1) and slow, deliberate thinking (System 2). Our eyes sample the environment in snapshots, and what we actually see depends heavily on where we focus our attention. Selective attention means we often miss things outside our focus, and our perception of color and depth can be influenced by lighting and context.

Visual processing involves both immediate, unconscious reactions (pre-attentive perception) and slower, conscious analysis (post-attentive perception). Gestalt principles explain how we naturally group visual elements, and certain visual attributes stand out instantly to help us quickly understand what we see.

Understanding these concepts is essential for designing effective visuals and improving how we interpret the world around us.
